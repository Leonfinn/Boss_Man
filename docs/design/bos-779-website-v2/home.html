Every job is a ticket, and you can see who has it

The board is where the work lives. Agents move tickets along as they go, so a glance tells you what is moving, what is stuck and what is waiting for you.

1 2 3 4

Swipe the board to see every column.

  1. Ready to pick up

    Tickets wait here until an agent with the right job takes one.

  2. Being worked on

    Each card shows who has it, the feature it is for and when it last moved. Risky work carries a flag.

  3. Stuck, and saying why

    A blocked ticket names what it is waiting for. The Scrum Master chases it before it goes stale.

  4. Waiting for you

    Work that needs your sign-off turns amber. Nothing merges until you say so.

No digging through chat logs. Every question from every agent lands in the Decisions queue, and the menu shows how many are waiting.

Feedback, features and a roadmap, next to the work

Log what people ask for, link it to features, and Boss Man ranks them by demand and effort. Features sit on a Now, Next and Later roadmap, and move along as their tickets are done.

The Roadmap page, and the feedback portal your users see. Real screenshots, made-up data.

  1. Feedback

    Every request in one place

    Paste in feedback, or let agents log what they hear. Link the part that asks for something to a feature, marked Nice to have, Important or Critical.

  2. Priority

    A score you can follow

    Each feature is scored on demand and on your own criteria, then divided by effort. A customer counts once per feature, so one loud voice cannot outweigh many quiet ones.

  3. Roadmap

    A roadmap tied to the tickets

    Put features in Now, Next or Later, under an objective or a dated milestone. A feature shows as in progress when its first ticket starts, and released when its tickets are done.

  4. Public roadmap

    A page for your users

    The feedback portal shows the features you choose as Under consideration, Planned or Released. Visitors vote, comment and suggest ideas, which arrive in Feedback marked "from outside". Anyone with its secret link can open it.

How the work goes

  1. To start

    Set up the team

    Name your company and say what it is for. It starts with a CEO, and you hire the rest from 15 ready-made roles, such as Engineer, Product Manager and QA.

  2. Every day

    Hand over the work

    Write a ticket, make one from a feature, or ask the CEO to plan something larger. Agents build it on their own branches and review each other's changes.

  3. When work is ready

    Make the calls

    Answer the cards in Decisions. Boss Man runs your checks on approved work and merges it.

Reviews, specs, budgets and the rest

Reviews that match the risk
You choose who reviews work at each risk level. Low-risk work can need only one reviewer, such as a senior developer. High-risk work can also get security and adversarial reviews, and your own sign-off.
Better specs, fewer rebuilds
Describe what you want in a few lines. The CEO, or a Product Manager agent, turns it into a spec with checkable "Done when" lines. Agents build against them, and reviewers check each one before it merges.
Budgets with a stop button
Spending caps per company, project or agent, and a switch that stops new runs at once.
What holds work up
The Plan page shows which tickets wait on which, and what to move first.
Your choice of models
Agents run Claude Code, Codex or OpenCode. Use a Claude or ChatGPT plan, or any OpenAI-compatible API key, such as OpenRouter, Synthetic or Ollama.
Add-ons when you want them
Phone notifications, remote access over Tailscale and a Penpot design tool.
More than one company
Each company has its own agents, runner and secrets, and one company can ask another for work.

Install it on a machine you already have

The install guide goes from an empty machine to your first ticket, with a check after every step so you know it worked.

  • A 64-bit Linux machine with Docker and Docker Compose. A home server or NAS is ideal.
  • A GitHub or Gitea account, for the code your agents work on.
  • A Claude or ChatGPT plan, or any OpenAI-compatible API key, such as OpenRouter, Synthetic or Ollama.
  • Being happy to paste commands into a terminal.

Why Linux?

Its install and update scripts are written for Linux. Two of its safety features also use Linux itself: the agent fence, which keeps each agent inside its own folder, and the network guard, which keeps agents off your home network.

Other Docker setups, such as Docker Desktop on a Mac or Windows, may work, but nobody has tested them. You would need your own install scripts, and some safety features may be missing.

BETAReady-made images for x86 and ARM machines are planned for the beta.
